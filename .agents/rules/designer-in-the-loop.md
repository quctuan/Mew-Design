# The designer judges; approved work is not yours to change

Three habits that a long motion or UI project taught the hard way. They apply to every track.

## 1. Ask before changing approved work

Once the designer has approved a frame, reference, timing, or idea, do not replace or "improve" it on your own. Ask first, in one or two sentences: what you want to change and why. A redo is cheap when asked about and expensive when discovered.

- A fix that drifts away from an approved reference counts as a change. Compare against the reference before and after.
- If you cannot deliver an approved element as specified, say so and offer options. Do not deliver a different one silently.
- A decision you made yourself because the brief was silent is not an approval. Log it and surface it (see `design-tracks`, build plan).
- Prefer a new labeled variant beside the approved work over an edit in place, unless the designer asked for the edit.

**Why:** in one onboarding project the only full redo came from rewriting an approved reference without asking.

## 2. Diagnose before fixing

When the designer says "sloppy", "cheap", "boring", "jerky" or "rough", that is a symptom, not a brief. Before changing anything:

1. Name specific, checkable causes, ideally three to six. Measure where you can (frame timing, spacing, type scale, number of simultaneous movers).
2. Say which cause you think matters most and why.
3. Fix the causes, then re-check the same measurements.

A restyle that does not name its causes tends to swap one problem for another. If the designer rejects a proposal, check that it does not contradict the diagnosis you already agreed on.

**Why:** "sloppy, not professional" became six concrete issues, and the next take fixed all six. A blind "make it jerk less" build got worse on every number that was measured.

## 3. The designer is the judge of feel, taste, and sound

Tools verify structure and numbers: geometry, bindings, reaction graphs, contrast, frame timing, pixel diffs, audio offsets. They cannot verify how motion feels, how a mix sounds, or whether a design looks cheap.

- Never say a motion or audio result "works" or "feels right". Say what you measured and hand the judgment back: "Timing matches the spec to 5 ms. Please play it and tell me how it feels."
- Give the designer a way to judge quickly: a link, a time-seek URL, a Play button, an APK.
- Treat the designer's words ("boring", "rough", "collides") as data. Record them next to the change that answered them.
